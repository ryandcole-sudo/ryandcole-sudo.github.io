    Slicing Circles: How Kepler Solved Planetary Motion

    📅 Published: August 15, 2026 ⏱️ 26 min read 👤 Ryan Cole

    Suppose we want to slice a given circle into two segments using a chord. If our goal is to divide the circle so that the areas of the two segments have a specified ratio, where along the diameter should we make the cut?

    Conceptually, this is not a very difficult geometric problem. Let the circle have radius $r=1$ (a unit circle), and let $x$ be the distance from the center to the cut. Construct a triangle using the chord and the center of the circle. This is an isosceles triangle, and we can easily show that its height is $x=\cos\dfrac\theta2$ and its base is $y=\sin\dfrac\theta2$. This follows directly from the inscribed angle theorem and the unit circle definitions of $\sin$ and $\cos$. The area of the triangle is therefore: $$A=\cos\frac\theta2\sin\frac\theta2$$ Applying the double angle formula for $\sin$ simplifies this to: $$A=\frac12\sin\theta$$

    Notice also that the triangle and the segment together form a sector. For a unit circle, the area of the sector equals half the angle in radians. To find the area of the segment, we subtract the triangle’s area from the sector’s area: $$A=\frac12\left(\theta-\sin\theta\right)$$ This equation is a variant of Kepler’s equation. Kepler first derived it in his Astronomia Nova while studying the orbit of Mars. But wait—what does slicing circles have to do with planetary orbits? To answer that, I think it will help to look at how Kepler arrived at his laws of planetary motion.

    Origin of Kepler’s Laws

    Kepler’s groundbreaking work on planetary motion owes much to work of his predecessors. Most importantly to Copernicus who created the heliocentric system, and also to Ptolemy, Almagest provides the source for many of the geometric techniques essential for analyzing planetary motion.

    Kepler’s work also depended heavily on Tycho Brahe’s extensive planetary observations, and particularly those of Mars, whose motion proved especially difficult to reconcile with existing theories.

    Geometric Tools for Analyzing Planetary Motion

    Since the time of the Greeks there were two basic geometric tools that were in use for analyzing the motion of planets. Copernicus, and later Kepler adopts this to these techniques to the heliocentric system. These are the opposition and elongation method. Kepler also introduced another method using orbital inclination.

    At opposition, a planet lies exactly opposite the Sun as seen from Earth, so the Earth, planet, and Sun form a straight line. Consequently, the observed position of the planet from Earth at opposition equals its angular position as seen from the Sun. In other words, oppositions give us a direct view of the planet from the Sun’s perspective.

    When the planet is not at opposition, elongation occurs, and the Earth, planet, and Sun form a triangle. Since knowing two angles and one side of any triangle lets us determine the rest, we can use this technique to measure distances. From Earth we directly observe the angle between the Sun and the planet, while the angle at the Sun—between Earth and planet—is usually derived from a theory of planetary motion. Once we have both angles, we can determine the relative distances of Earth and the planet from the Sun.

    In the diagram. The elongation angles are represented by $e_\mathrm{Earth}$ , $e_\mathrm{Planet}$ and $e_\mathrm{Sun}$ , and the distances to the sun are given by $d_\mathrm{Earth}$ and $d_\mathrm{Planet}$. We can show using the sine rule that $$\frac{\sin(e_\mathrm{Earth})}{d_\mathrm{Planet} } = \frac{\sin(e_\mathrm{Planet})}{d_\mathrm{Earth} } $$ We can also express this as $$\frac{\sin(e_\mathrm{Earth})}{d_\mathrm{Planet} } = \frac{\sin(e_\mathrm{Sun}+e_\mathrm{Earth})}{d_\mathrm{Earth} } $$

    In addition to these two primary tools, Kepler introduced another method: orbital inclination. It was already known that planets do not orbit in the same plane, but Kepler was the first to show—using accurate data from Tycho Brahe—that all planets orbit in a fixed plane relative to each other (i.e., the orbits do not wobble), and that these planes always intersect at a common point: the Sun. For Kepler, this result demonstrated that the Sun was not merely at the center to provide light, but was physically involved in planetary motion.

    IIn the diagram, the angle $i$ denotes the constant orbital inclination. The angle $\bar\beta = 180^\circ - \beta$ is the supplement of the ecliptic latitude $\beta$ . The ecliptic latitude is defined as the angle between Mars and the plane of the ecliptic—Earth’s orbital plane—as observed from Earth. Using the sine rule, $$\frac{\sin(\bar\beta)}{d_\mathrm{Planet}}=\frac{\sin(i+\bar\beta)}{d_\mathrm{Earth}}.$$ which we can express directly in terms of $\beta$ as $$\frac{\sin(\beta)}{d_\mathrm{Planet}}=\frac{\sin(i-\beta)}{d_\mathrm{Earth}}$$

    Planetary Models inherited by Kepler

    When we observe the motion of a planet from sun, using opposition data, we notice that the motion is not uniform. The planet is faster in one half of its orbit, and slower in the other half. Greek astronomers deduced a very simple model that could fit the data with reasonable accuracy. This model was later adopted into the heliocentric system in the following form: the planet orbits in uniform circular motion, and the orbit is not centered on the sun. This model is called the eccentric circle model. The point where the planet reaches closest to the sun is the perihelion, and the point on the opposite side, where the planet is furthest from the sun, is the aphelion. The eccentricity is defined as the distance between the center of the circle and the sun divided by the radius of the orbit.

    Hipparchus demonstrated a method whereby the parameters could be found from just three opposition observations. The eccentric circle model was able to fit the data for most planets reasonably well, but it was not sufficiently accurate. The error became even more obvious when one tried to use this theory to determine distances to the planet using the elongation method. The distances were simply not consistent with what was predicted by the uniform circular model.

    However, when they investigated further, they noticed a pattern: the distances are slightly smaller than expected when measured closer to aphelion and greater when measured closer to perihelion. It was as if the real center of the circle was closer to the sun than what the uniform circular model supposed.

    Ptolemy suggested exactly this fix when he created the equant model. The point from which uniform circular motion is determined is called the equant. Ptolemy’s suggestion when adopted into the heliocentric system, states that the real center of the circle lies halfway between the equant and the sun. Not only did this fix the distances, but it matched the observations at oppositions with great accuracy. The equant is often hailed as one of the greatest inventions of ancient astronomy.

    In the diagram, the dashed circle shows where the planet would be if it moved in uniform motion. The center of this circle is the equant and is colored black on the diagram. The solid circle is the one on which the planet actually moves, and its center is colored blue on the diagram.

    In this model, we define three important angles that are all measured with respect to perihelion: the mean anomaly, defined as the angle at the equant between the planet and perihelion; the eccentric anomaly, defined as the angle at the center of the circle between the planet and perihelion; and the true anomaly, defined as the angle at the Sun between the planet and perihelion.

    The mean anomaly is proportional to time, since the motion is uniform around the equant point. Let $M$ be the mean anomaly; let $\omega$ be the angular speed of the planet; let $t$ be time and let $M_0$ represent the initial value of mean anomaly, then we have: $$M=\omega t+M_0$$ To find the relationship between eccentric anomaly and mean anomaly, we can zoom in on the triangle formed between the equant, the center and the planet. We assume that the orbit of the planet is a unit circle $r=1$. The eccentricity is then the distance between the center and the equant.

    In the diagram, $E’=180-E$ is the supplement of the eccentric anomaly, and $M$ is the mean anomaly. The other angle in the triangle is $180-(E’+M)$, which can also be simplified as $E-M$. Using the sine rule to relate opposite sides of the triangle, we thus obtain the relationship: $$\sin(E-M)=e\sin M$$ We can easily solve this for $E$ given $M$.

    To find the relationship between true anomaly and eccentric anomaly, we zoom in on the triangle formed between the sun, the center, and the planet. $\tau’=180=\tau$ is the supplementary angle of the true anomaly, $E$ is the eccentric anomaly. Much like the previous case, we find the other angle in the triangle as $180-(\tau’+E)$, which simplifies to $\tau-E$. We can then use the sine rule to find $$\sin(\tau-E) =e\sin(\tau)$$ We can also use this same triangle to find the distance of the planet from the sun. Using the cosine rule, we find the distance $d$ from the sun as: $$d=\sqrt{1+e^2-2e\cos(E)}$$

    Vicarious Hypothesis

    Ptolemy’s assumption that the center of the circle lies halfway between the equant and the sun worked well for a long time. When Tycho Brahe began collecting very accurate data on the planets, he used a variant of this model to fit his observations, and it worked well, except for a small but notable error in the orbit of Mars. He tasked his assistants with developing a more accurate model for its orbit.

    The most straightforward suggestion was to drop the assumption that the circle’s center lies halfway between the equant and the sun. Kepler initially considered this model. Let $e$ be the distance between the sun and the center of the circle (the eccentricity), and let $q$ be the distance between the equant and the center of the circle. In the previous model, we assumed $e=q$. We now consider the more general case where $e\neq q$ .

    The derivations of the relationships among mean anomaly, eccentric anomaly, and true anomaly follow the same steps as before, but now with $e\neq q$. Thus, we obtain the following equations for a general equant model. $$\sin(E-M)=q\sin(M)$$ $$\sin(\tau-E)=e\sin(\tau)$$ $$d=\sqrt{1+e^2-2e\cos(E)}$$

    Solving for the parameters

    To find the parameters for the model, Kepler considers a set of opposition observations of the planet, for which we are given the time $t$ and position in ecliptic longitude $\ell$ . The mean anomaly of each observation can be found as $M=\omega t + M_0$, and the true anomaly is found by subtracting the constant $\bar{\alpha}$ (the longitude of perihelion) from the ecliptic longitude: $\tau=\ell-\bar\alpha$ (this follows from the definition of true anomaly).

    If we could guess values for $M_0$ and $\bar\alpha$, we could use them to find the mean and true anomaly for all the observations. Then, with these angles, we could plot the position of the planet using the triangle formed between the Sun, equant, and planet. The base of this triangle is given by $e+q$. The angles are the mean anomaly $M$ and the the supplement of the true anomaly $\tau’=180-\tau$. The other angle in the triangle is given by $180-(\tau’+M)$ which is $\tau-M$. We can now use the sine rule to relate the angles and find the distance: $$d=\frac{(e+q)\sin M}{\sin(\tau-M)}$$ For each observation, we can then plot the position of the planet in radial coordinates, since we have its distance from the sun $d$ and angle from the sun, the true anomaly $\tau$. The goal is to find values for $M_0$ and $\bar\alpha$ such that the observations all land on a circle and the center of this circle falls on the line connecting the equant and the sun. Because any three non-collinear points determine a circle, testing whether observations fit a circle requires at least four observations.

    First, we adjust $\bar\alpha$ until the observations form a circle. Next, we adjust $M_0$ to bring the circle’s center onto the line connecting the equant and the sun. But this second adjustment can disrupt the circular shape, so we iterate between the two steps until both conditions hold simultaneously: a circular orbit whose center lies between the equant and the sun. The location of that center then gives us the eccentricities $e$ and $q$.

    Results

    After carrying out this long iterative calculation by hand for Mars, Kepler obtained the values for $e=0.113$, $q=0.073$, a near 3:2 ratio between the eccentricities. He further demonstrated that the resulting model fits the rest of Tycho’s opposition observations accurate to within the observational limits, which Kepler estimated to be 2 arcminutes (0.03 degrees). He referred to this model as the vicarious hypothesis.

    For comparison, he considered what would happen if we converted this into a bisected equant model. If we take the sum of the eccentricities $e+q=0.186$, and divide by 2, we get a model with $e=q=0.093$. The bisected equant model fits the data, but with errors of up to 8 arcminutes (0.13 degrees). This was significant enough for Kepler to reject the bisected equant model.

    Plotting the Orbit of the Earth

    Accurately determining distances to planets using the elongation method requires knowing the distance from the Earth to the Sun. But there is no easy way to determine this distance directly. To make matters worse, because the Earth’s eccentricity is small, it made no difference whether astronomers used an equant or simple uniform circular motion model to predict the longitudes, as the difference in prediction was smaller than the observational errors.

    From Tycho’s data, it could be shown the that the Earth’s eccentricity sum $e+q=0.036$. Tycho used a uniform circular orbit model $e=0.036$, $q=0$, because it fit the observations well enough. Kepler showed that a bisected equant model with $e=q=0.018$ would fit the observations equally well, but obviously both models would predict different values for the Earth–sun distances. How could we tell which one is correct then?

    Kepler came up with a clever method for computing Earth distances based on Mars observations. After Mars completes one full revolution in its orbit, it returns to the same position in its orbit, and the same distance from the Sun. Kepler takes several observations spaced apart $687$ days, which is the orbital period of Mars 1.

    The idea is to then use the elongation method in reverse to compute Earth distances relative to Mars. For each observation of Mars, the elongation angle $e_\mathrm{Earth}$ is determined directly by observing the Sun and Mars from Earth. The angle is given by $e_\mathrm{Sun}=\ell_\mathrm{Mars}-\ell_\mathrm{Earth}$, where the longitudes $\ell_\mathrm{Earth}$ and $\ell_\mathrm{Mars}$ come directly from theory. For Mars we use the vicarious hypothesis and for Earth we may use simple uniform motion.

    Once we have distances, we can pair them with the longitudes to plot the position of Earth. With three or more such points, we can then find the circular orbit which best fits the observations. After finding this circle and locating its center, Kepler was able to directly determine the eccentricity of the Earth’s orbit; he got a value very close to $e=0.018$, which agrees with the bisected equant model.

    To test the consistency of his results, Kepler found another set of observations of Mars separated by $687$ days. This time he computed the position of Mars for each of the observations, and found that the same value for each observation.

    Plotting the Orbit of Mars

    With the eccentricity of the Earth now determined, Kepler could use this information to find accurate distances for the Earth, which could then be used to determine accurate distances to other planets using elongation or latitude methods.

    Kepler takes several observations of Mars spread across its orbit, and used the elongation method to find the distances to Mars, then he fits the result to a circular orbit, and locates the distance of its center from the sun to find the eccentricity of Mars’ orbit. Doing this, he finds a value very close to $e=0.92$ in perfect agreement with the bisected equant model.

    This result contradicts that of the vicarious hypothesis from earlier, where we established a near $3:2$ ratio between the eccentricities using the opposition data. The discrepancy forces us to question the underlying equant model used for the computing the parameters the vicarious hypothesis

    Kepler examines the error in the oppositions and notes that the error seems greatest for observations that were closer to the octants (where the mean anomaly is close to $45\degree$,$135\degree$, $225\degree$ or $315\degree$). The vicarious hypothesis—which agrees with the opposition observations—shows the planet ahead of the predictions of the bisected equant model at $45\degree$ and $225\degree$, while behind at $135\degree$ and $315\degree$. The data suggests that the true anomaly of Mars is increasing faster than expected around aphelion and perihelion, while increasing slower than expected half-way between perihelion and aphelion.

    The Law of Distances

    Kepler now suspects that the equant law is at fault here and he seeks to find a new law to replace it. The effect of an equant is to change the speed of the planet in its orbit. Since equal angles measured from the equant correspond to equal time intervals, the planet must cover a larger distance in the same time when it is farther from the equant.

    Alternatively, the situation can be viewed from the point of view of the sun.  Because the Sun lies opposite the equant, the reverse occurs: the planet moves faster when it is closer to the sun, and slows down when it is further away. Kepler believes this is the real law underlying planetary motion and the equant is only a useful approximation to it that has worked up to this point.

    Kepler defines the distance law more concretely this way: the time a planet takes to travel a given arc is directly proportional to the length of the arc multiplied by the planet’s average distance from the Sun along that arc. Since this distance is always changing, Kepler estimated the average distance by splitting the arc into many small pieces. He determined the distance to the Sun for each piece, added the distances together, then divided by the number of pieces. He then divides that result by the average distance over the entire orbit, which gives the time as a fraction of the orbital period.

     Let us recall from earlier the formula we derived for the distance planet from the sun in an eccentric circular orbit as a function of the eccentric anomaly $E$.

    $$d(E)=\sqrt{1+e^2-2e\cos(E)}$$

    Here the eccentric anomaly $E$ represents the arc along the circle from perihelion to the planet, and $e$ is the eccentricity. Kepler’s procedure is divide the circle to $n$ very small arcs, each having an arclength of $\dfrac{2\pi}{n}$. The eccentric anomaly at the $i^\mathrm{th}$ arc is $E_i=i\cdot\dfrac{2\pi}n$. We find the average of the distances times the eccentric anomaly as $$\left(\dfrac{\sum_{k=0}^i d(E_k)}i\right)\cdot i\cdot \frac{2\pi}n$$ which simplifies to $$\frac{2\pi}n\sum_{k=0}^i d(E_k)$$ The average distance over the whole circle is given as $$\frac{\sum_{k=0}^n d(E_k)}{n}$$ The ratio of these two quantities give the mean anomaly $M$ $$M=2\pi\frac{\sum_{k=0}^id(E_k)}{\sum_{k=0}^n d(E_k)}$$ This formula replaces the formula for computing mean anomaly using the equant in the vicarious hypothesis from earlier. Since the there is no equant, the mean anomaly no longer represents a meaningful angle, but rather a convenient way of expressing time. The formulas for computing the true anomaly and distance remain the same since the only depend on the circle and not the equant.

    If this formula looks intimidating, imagine how much more so it must have been for Kepler in 1604. Calculus had not yet been invented, and logarithmic tables were still a few years away. In order to compute just a single value for the mean anomaly,  he would have to work through an extremely long and painstaking sum just to obtain the denominator. If he needed to tweak the the eccentricity—or if he made a single mistake—he would have to start the calculation all over again. Kepler desperately needed a shortcut.

    The Law of Areas

    While dividing the circle into tiny sectors to carry out the calculation, Kepler noticed that each small sector is approximately a triangle, with its base equal to the arc length and its height equal to the radius. He recalled that Archimedes had used this idea to find the area of a circle. Since a triangle’s area is half the base times the height, the area of the circle becomes $\frac12\times(2\pi r)\times r = \pi r^2$.

     Kepler now applies the same reasoning to a sector swept out from the Sun rather than from the center of a circle. If we approximate this sector as a triangle, the height would be the distance from the planet to the Sun, $d(E_k)$, and the base would be the arc length $E_k$. The area of this triangle is $\frac12 \times d(E_k)\times E_k$. Twice this value gives an approximation to the distance law. If this approximation holds, we conclude that the mean anomaly (or time) is proportional to the area swept out from the Sun to the planet.  

    In the diagram we have a unit circle, the sector drawn from the center has an area equal to $\frac12 E$, following Archimedes’ proof, and the area of the sector drawn from the sun is $\frac12 M$, based on the distance law. The green triangle in the diagram has a base equal to the eccentricity $e$, and one side equal to radius $1$ with the internal angle equal to $E$. We can thus show that the height of this triangle is $\sin E$, and therefore that the area is equal to $\frac12 e \sin E$ . We can use the diagram then to express geometrical relationship with the following equation $$\frac12M=\frac12E -\frac12e\sin E$$ which simplifies further to $$M=E-e\sin E$$ This was a much easier formula to work with. He could now compute the value in a single step. But for Kepler the area law was only ever meant to be an approximation to the true law of distances. He could already show that for a sufficiently high eccentricity orbit, the approximation would no longer work because the base of the triangle is no longer parallel to the arc length creating a small difference in area that adds up. However, for practical orbits this difference was small enough to be ignored.

    Comparing the Area Law with the Observations

    Using the law of areas, Kepler procedes to compare the result the of the true anomaly calculated from a circular model of $e=0.092$ to the predictions of the vicarious hypothesis, he finds that the predictions are once again in error. The error is about the same as before (8 arcminutes) at the same places, but now in the complete opposite direction to what we had when comparing the bisected equant model. The data now suggests that the true anomaly is increasing slower than expected at perihelion and aphelion and faster at the sides.

    The Orbit of Mars is not a Circle

    Kepler procedes to once again plot the orbit of Mars using the elongation method. He finds several observations and tries to fit a circle to it. He then compares the distance of the points from the circle, and some of the results are far enough, when compared to the accuracy of the data to suggest that the orbit is probably not a circle.

    To investigate this result more carefully, Kepler comes up with a technique: he gather several observations close to perihelion and several observations close to aphelion. He uses this to plot the position of Mars near these points so he can get an accurate estimate for the perihelion and aphelion. Now he draws the circle through perihelion and aphelion. Doing it this way allows him to see exactly where the orbit falls short of a circle. The data shows that the planet’s path is symmetric on both sides of the perihelion, but pulls in more closer at the sides.

    This result also vindicates the law of areas, because if we consider what would happen to Mars if it pulls in closer to the sun, it would move faster, which is consistent with the error we got when comparing the area law to the vicarious hypothesis. The speed predictions of the bisected equant model on the other hand would suggest an orbit that is bulged out at the sides instead.

    The Vicarious Egg

    Kepler now considers what reasonable physical mechanism could cause the planet to move on a non-circular orbit. Kepler is working before the invention of Newton’s laws, so he has none of that. He considers the following idea: imagine that the natural tendency of the sun to move planets in a circular path, and the planet also has its own tendency to move in a circular path. When combined, the effect is like the motion of an epicycle on a deferent. If the we make the radius of the epicycle equal to the eccentricity $e$, and the planet moves uniformly on the epicycle, then it can be shown that a simple epicycle creates an eccentric circle in which case the distance formula would be the same as we had before. $$d =\sqrt{1+e^2-2e\cos(M)}$$ The formula uses mean anomaly because the motion on the epicycle is uniform. However, the speed of the planet itself is not uniform. The angle from the sun—the true anomaly—must be computed directly from the law of areas or equivalently by summing the distances. Because we use a different formula for the angle than what a circular orbit would require, the result is an oval shape.

    The shape is controlled by the speed of the planet, so at perihelion, where the planet moves faster the oval is slightly narrower, and it is slightly wider at the aphelion. Kepler doubt this the egg model. The effect however is exceedingly small, and so for all practical measurement the shape can be regarded as symmetric. The diagram below shows a slightly exaggerated version of this.

    The problem for Kepler however was the difficulty in constructing this egg mathematically. Because it was difficult to find the exact relationship between mean and eccentric anomaly for the shape without first being able to construct the shape. Working before the invention of calculus, Kepler simply didn’t have the mathematical tools necessary to carry out this calculation.

    Kepler had the idea to use the vicarious hypothesis. This gives correct values for the true anomaly. Then he plugs the corresponding mean anomaly values into the formula to find the result. Plotting a few points like this allows him to get a reasonable approximation for the shape of the curve. But when he compares it to the observations, he realizes that the curve comes too far in at the sides, twice the amount that the observation requires.

    Radial Oscillation on the Orbit

    The failure of the previous model forced Kepler to reason up another physical hypothesis that could account for the non-circular orbit. He considers the following: imagine planets are magnetic in nature. The sun naturally works to rotate the planet around in circular motion, however when one magnetic pole faces the sun, the planet is pushed towards the sun; when the opposite pole faces the sun, the planet is pulled away from the sun. And in general, when the magnetic pole is at an angle to the sun, the strength of the force is given as the cosine of that angle. The result is the planet oscillates along the radius of the circle, and this oscillation gives the distance of the planet from the sun.

    We imagine a unit circle with the sun at the center. We suppose that the planet oscillates along the radius line connecting it to the sun. The oscillation occurs in such a way that the distance from the sun is given by the formula $$d=1-e\cos E$$ where $E$ is the eccentric anomaly and $e$ is the eccentricity.

    This model is great. Kepler was able to show that the distances computed this way match perfectly to the observations.

    The Orbit is an Ellipse

    The surprising result, which Kepler would eventually show is that the path generated by this oscillating motion is a perfect ellipse.

    Properties of an ellipse

    An ellipse was originally defined as one of the curves that results from slicing a cone, where the cut is made at an oblique angle to that cone.

    Archimedes later showed that an ellipse can also result from scaling a circle along a single axis. This result made it rather easy to determine the area of an ellipse: the scale factor multiplied by the area of the original circle. The same scaling also applies to any sector or segment drawn on the original circle.

    However, scaling does not affect arcs and lines drawn on the circle in the same way. The effect of scaling depends on the orientation of each relative to the scaling axis. Lines and arcs that are parallel to the scaling axis are affected more than those that are perpendicular to it.

    Law of Distances Applied to Ellipse

    For Kepler, the real law of planetary motion was always the law of distances. The area law was only intended as an approximation to this law. It worked well for circles, but it was clear that the same approximation was not going to work for ellipses. Because if we scale a circle, the area of any of the sectors always scales proportionally, but the distances and arcs are not affected uniformly by scaling. Recall that to find the mean anomaly using the distance law, we divide the ellipse into small arcs and sum the product of each distance and its arc length. Now this product will only scale proportionally if the arcs and distances are perpendicular to each other. The easiest way to see this is to note that when they are perpendicular, their product corresponds exactly to the area of a rectangle; if they are not perpendicular, that simple geometric correspondence no longer holds.

    Let us draw a circle with a unit radius, and inside it we have drawn an ellipse generated by scaling the circle by a factor $b$ along one of its axis. For the original circle, we have

    $$r=\sqrt{\sin^2 E+\cos^2E}=1$$ We see from the diagram, that scaling factor applies to the sine axis, so that the distances for the ellipse become: $$r=\sqrt{b\sin^2E+\cos^2E}$$

    Recall that earlier we had found the distances for a point off-center from the circle, by using the cosine rule applied to the triangle formed between the center, the eccentric point and the ellipse, in that case we had found the distance is given by $$d =\sqrt{1+e^2-2e\cos(E)}$$

    In the diagram, we have set up a similar scenario with an eccentric point on the ellipse. Using the cosine rule, and the distance formula we found earlier for the lines from the center, we derive the formula for the distances from the eccentric point on the ellipse as $$d =\sqrt{b\sin^2E+\cos^2E+e^2-2e\cos(E)}$$ Now if it so happened that the eccentric point was chosen, so that $e^2=1-b^2$ , then we can show that the distance formula simplifies dramatically. Let’s make the substitution $b^2=1-e^2$ into our original expression to get:

    $$d=\sqrt{(1-e^2)\sin^2 E+\cos^2 E+e^2-2e\cos E}$$ which becomes $$d=\sqrt{\sin^2E-e^2\sin^2E+\cos^2E+e^2-2e\cos E}$$ we can simplify further by using $\sin^2E+\cos^2 E = 1$ to get $$d=\sqrt{1+e^2-e^2\sin E-2e\cos E}$$ We then factor out the $e^2$ term and use the identity $\cos^2 E = 1 - \sin^2 E$ to obtain $$d=\sqrt{1+e^2\cos^2 E-2e\cos E}$$ The final step is to simply note that this becomes a perfect square, so that $$d=\sqrt{(1-e\cos E)^2}$$ and hence we obtain the remarkably simple formula for computing the distances for an eccentric point on an ellipse as: $$d=1-e\cos E$$ This is also the very same expression which Kepler derived earlier in his radial oscillation theory. In one stroke, he proved that a planet’s radial oscillation produces elliptical motion, with the sun at a special point on the ellipse—the focus. Kepler had introduced the term ‘focus’ in his work on optics. Apollonius had discovered two points for which the sum of distances to any point on the ellipse is constant, but had not named them; Kepler called them foci. However, he didn’t realize until a few years later that this focus was the same as the sun’s location.

    Finding the True Anomaly for an Ellipse

    The true anomaly can be found by applying the cosine rule to the triangle formed between $e$, $d$ and the radius of the original circle $1$.

    From the diagram we can see that the angle $\bar{\tau}=180-\tau$ is the supplementary angle for the true anomaly $$\cos\bar{\tau} = \frac{e^2+d^2-1}{2ed}$$ We can then substitute the distance $d=1-\cos E$ and $\cos\bar\tau=-\cos\tau$ to obtain

    $$-\cos\tau=\frac{e^2+(1+e^2-2e\cos E)-1}{2e(1-\cos E)}$$ This then simplifies down quite nicely to $$\cos\tau=\frac{\cos E-e}{1-e \cos E}$$

    1. Computing the orbital period is not difficult. Since ancient times, we have recorded that a total of 37 oppositions of Mars occur in a period of nearly exactly 79 years. Oppositions occur whenever the Earth’s orbit catches up with Mars. This means that when the Earth completes 79 orbits it passes Mars 37 times, meaning Mars only completed $79-37=42$ orbits in that same period. This gives us the ratio of the orbital period for Earth and Mars $\dfrac{79}{42}\approx 1.88$ years. If we take an Earth year as $365.25$ days, that gives the orbital period of Mars as $687$ days. ↩︎
