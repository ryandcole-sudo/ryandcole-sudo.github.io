    Accuracy of the Epicycle Model for Predicting Eclipses

    📅 Published: September 22, 2026 ⏱️ 28 min read 👤 Ryan Cole

    Over 2,000 years ago, the Greek astronomer Hipparchus (c. 150 BC) developed one of the first geometric models to predict the motion of the Sun and Moon. In this model, the Moon moves on a small circle called the epicycle, which itself moves on a larger circle called the deferent. Both these motions are uniform. The main reason for introducing this model was to explain the observed non-uniform motion. The ancient Babylonian astronomers had noticed that the moon sometimes moved faster across the sky than at other times. In the previous article, I looked at how the Babylonians deduced this, and also how they accurately calculated the various periods of the lunar orbit.

    The epicycle model offers a geometric explanation for the Moon’s varying speed: when the Moon moves forward on its epicycle, it appears to accelerate; when it moves backward, it slows down. The Moon is at perigee when it reaches its closest point to Earth on the epicycle and at apogee when it is farthest. Hipparchus chose the epicycle to move in the same direction as the deferent, so that the Moon appears fastest at perigee and slowest at apogee.

    Ancient astronomers also recognized that the Moon’s path is slightly inclined to the Sun’s path. The two points where these paths intersect are called the nodes, and they drift slowly backward against the Moon’s motion. This explained eclipses, which can occur only when the Moon crosses the Sun’s path at a node. This is the mechanism that gives the explanation for why eclipses occur: they occur when the Moon is close to a node.

    Hipparchus’ addition of the epicycle made for much more accurate prediction of eclipses than simple uniform motion could provide. My goal in this article is to determine how accurate this epicycle model is at predicting eclipses. First, however, we must show how the best parameters for the model can be derived from observations.

    How to Determine the Parameters for the Model

    In the epicycle model, there are two motions to consider: the motion of the epicycle itself around the Earth, and the motion of the moon on its epicycle. The epicycle is said to make one complete revolution around the Earth when its center returns to the same position on the deferent circle. The moon is said to complete one revolution on its epicycle when it returns to the same position as seen from the center of the deferent. For example, if the moon starts at apogee on the epicycle, then when it reaches apogee again it is said to complete one revolution on the epicycle.

    The speed of the moon and its epicycle can be determined from the lengths of months. The previous article details how the Babylonian astronomers determined the various month lengths directly from observations.

    A tropical month is the time the Moon takes to return to the same position in the sky relative to the vernal equinox, a fixed reference point on the celestial sphere. In the epicycle model, the average length of the tropical month corresponds to the period in which the moon’s epicycle completes a full orbit around the deferent relative to the vernal equinox.

    An anomalistic month is the time the Moon takes to return to the same apparent speed (or anomaly) as seen from Earth. In the epicycle model, the average length of the anomalistic month corresponds to the period in which the Moon completes one full orbit on the epicycle relative to its apogee.

    The same concepts can be defined for the Sun, yielding the tropical and anomalistic years, which can likewise be used to set the parameters of an epicycle model for the Sun. Additionally, since the Moon and the Sun cross paths at the nodes, we can define the time each takes to return to the same node—these periods are known as the draconic month and the draconic year, respectively.

    Hipparchus’ Method

    To determine the radius of the Moon’s epicycle, and the Moon’s position on that epicycle at a given moment, Hipparchus needed only three observations of the Moon. For each one, he recorded the time it was made and the Moon’s position in its orbit as seen from Earth. I will now show how to arrive at this solution.

    The following diagram shows the geometric set-up for a planet on an epicycle. In the next diagram, we will zoom in on the triangle in the center and label its sides and angles.

    We assume a deferent of unit radius and an epicycle of radius $r$. The angle $\gamma$ is the observed position of the moon relative to the center of its epicycle, as seen from the Earth (the center of the deferent). This angle is referred to as the equation of center.

    The angle $\alpha$ is equal to the arc on the epicycle between the perigee and the Moon. This arc is called the epicyclic anomaly or mean anomaly.

    And the remaining angle $\beta$ is given by the fact that the angles in a triangle sum to $180\degree$. We thus have $\beta=180-\alpha-\gamma$.

    Then, we can use the law of sines, which gives the relation between two opposite angles to show: $$ \sin(\beta)=\dfrac{\sin(\gamma)}r$$

    If we take $\beta=180-\alpha-\gamma$ and apply the sine identity for supplementary angles, we rewrite the equation as:

    $$ r\sin(\alpha+\gamma)=\sin(\gamma)$$ This equation has three unknowns. To solve it, Hipparchus uses his three observations to create a system of three equations.

    The following diagram shows the positions $P_1$, $P_2$ and $P_3$ of the moon on the epicycle at each of the observations. The arcs $a_1$ and $a_2$ represent how far the moon travels along its epicycle between observations. Although we can’t see the moon moving on its epicycle, we know its speed. We can therefore use the elapsed time between observations to calculate the distance the moon must have traveled along the epicycle. Let the three observation times be $t_1$, $t_2$, and $t_3$, and let $\omega_e$ denote the moon’s speed on the epicycle. We then have

    $$a_1 =\omega_e(t_2-t_1)$$

    $$a_2=\omega_e(t_3-t_2)$$

    We can do a similar thing for the motion of the center of the epicycle on the deferent.

    For each observation, we are given the observed angle between the moon and a fixed point in the sky called the vernal equinox. This is angle called the ecliptic longitude. To determine these angles relative to the center of the epicycle, we must subtract the motion of the epicycle from the observed  motion in longitude. Let the ecliptic longitudes the three observations be $\lambda_1$, $\lambda_2$ and $\lambda_3$, and let $\omega_d$ denote the speed of the epicycle on the deferent. We then have the equations $$p_1 = (\lambda_2 - \lambda_1) - \omega_d(t_2-t_1)$$ $$p_2 =(\lambda_3-\lambda_2)-\omega_d(t_3-t_2)$$ $p_1$ and $p_2$ represent the observed motion of the moon across the sky as seen from the Earth, measured relative to the center of the epicycle. The quantities $a_1$ and $a_2$ are differences in the mean anomaly, and the quantities $p_1$ and $p_2$ are differences in the equation of center.

    Thus, if we say that at the observation $P_2$ the mean anomaly is $\alpha$, then the mean anomalies for $P_1$ and $P_3$ become $\alpha-a_1$ and $\alpha+a_2$ respectively. Likewise, if we say the equation of center at observation $P_2$ is $\gamma$, then the equation of center at $P_1$ and $P_3$ become $\gamma-p_1$ and $\gamma+p_2$ respectively.

    We can therefore write the equations corresponding to the three observations as

    $$r\sin((\alpha+\gamma)-(a_1+p_1)) = \sin(\gamma-p_1)$$ $$r\sin(\alpha+\gamma)=\sin(\gamma)$$ $$r\sin((\alpha+\gamma)+(a_2+p_2))=\sin(\gamma+p_2)$$

    This system of equations has three unknowns:  $r$, the epicycle radius; $\alpha$, the mean anomaly at the second observation; and $\gamma$, the equation of anomaly at the second observation.

    To solve this system, we first expand using the sine addition identity $\sin(u\pm v)= \sin(u)\cos(v)\pm \cos(u)\sin(v)$. This allows us to separate known terms ($a_1,a_2,p_1,p_2$) from the unknown terms ($\alpha$, $\gamma$ and $r$). After expanding, we get $$r\sin(\alpha+\gamma)\cos(a_1+p_1)-r\cos(\alpha+\gamma)\sin(a_1+p_1)=\sin(\gamma)\cos(p_1)-\cos(\gamma)\sin(p_1)$$ $$r\sin(\alpha+\gamma) = \sin(\gamma)$$ $$r\sin(\alpha+\gamma)\cos(a_2+p_2)+r\cos(\alpha+\gamma)\sin(a_2+p_2)= \sin(\gamma)\cos(p_2)+\cos(\gamma)\sin(p_2)$$

    Using the second equation, we can replace $r\sin(\alpha+\gamma)$ in first and third equations with $\sin(\gamma)$. This simplifies to the following two equations $$\sin(\gamma)\cos(a_1+p_1)-r\cos(\alpha+\gamma)\sin(a_1+p_1)=\sin(\gamma)\cos(p_1)-\cos(\gamma)\sin(p_1)$$ $$\sin(\gamma)\cos(a_2+p_2)+r\cos(\alpha+\gamma)\sin(a_2+p_2)= \sin(\gamma)\cos(p_2)+\cos(\gamma)\sin(p_2)$$

    For both of these equations, we now only need to rearrange and solve for $r\cos(\alpha+\gamma)$,

    $$r\cos(\alpha+\gamma)= \frac{\sin(\gamma)\cos(a_1+p_1)-\sin(\gamma)\cos(p_1)+\cos(\gamma)\sin(p_1)}{\sin(a_1+p_1)}$$ $$r\cos(\alpha+\gamma)= \frac{\sin(\gamma)\cos(p_2)+\cos(\gamma)\sin(p_2)-\sin(\gamma)\cos(a_2+p_2)}{\sin(a_2+p_2)}$$

    and we can now set them equal to each other: $$ \frac{\sin(\gamma)\cos(a_1+p_1)-\sin(\gamma)\cos(p_1)+\cos(\gamma)\sin(p_1)}{\sin(a_1+p_1)} = \frac{\sin(\gamma)\cos(p_2)+\cos(\gamma)\sin(p_2)-\sin(\gamma)\cos(a_2+p_2)}{\sin(a_2+p_2)} $$ Then we regroup, so that we have terms with $\cos(\gamma)$ on one side, and terms with $\sin(\gamma)$ on the other.

    $$\cos(\gamma)\left[ \frac{\sin(p_1)}{\sin(a_1+p_1)} -\frac{\sin(p_2)}{\sin(a_2+p_2)}\right]=\sin(\gamma)\left[ \frac{\cos(p_2)-\cos(a_2+p_2)}{\sin(a_2+p_2)} -\frac{\cos(a_1+p_1)-\cos(p_1)}{\sin(a_1+p_1)}\right]$$ Then we can cross-multiply to get: $$\tan(\gamma)=\frac{\sin(p_1)\sin(a_2+p_2)-\sin(p_2)\sin(a_1+p_1)}{\sin(a_1+p_1)\cos(p_2) - \sin(a_1+p_1)\cos(a_2+p_2) - \sin(a_2+p_2)\cos(a_1+p_1) + \sin(a_2+p_2)\cos(p_1)}$$ We can simplify the denominator a bit using the sine addition rule and regroup to get $$\tan(\gamma)=\frac{\sin(p_1)\sin(a_2+p_2)-\sin(p_2)\sin(a_1+p_1)}{\sin(a_1+p_1)\cos(p_2) + \sin(a_2+p_2)\cos(p_1) - \sin(a_1+a_2+p_1+p_2)}$$

    From this equation, we can compute the value for the equation of center $\gamma$. To find the the mean anomaly $\alpha$, we can use either of the formula we got for $r\cos(\alpha+\gamma)$ and the original second equation $r\sin(\alpha+\gamma)$ to find $$\tan(\alpha+\gamma)=\frac{\sin(\gamma)\sin(a_1+p_1)}{\sin(\gamma)\cos(a_1+p_1)-\sin(\gamma)\cos(p_1)+\cos(\gamma)\sin(p_1)}$$ We can also simplify the denominator here a bit to get: $$\tan(\alpha+\gamma)=\frac{\sin(\gamma)\sin(a_1+p_1)}{\sin(\gamma)\cos(a_1+p_1)+\sin(p_1-\gamma)}$$ Then we can find the radius of the epicycle $r$ from the second equation $$r = \frac{\sin(\gamma)}{\sin(\alpha+\gamma)} $$ Lastly, we can compute the mean longitude $\bar{\lambda}_2$ at the second observation—which represents the position of the center of the epicycle on the deferent, by subtracting the equation of center $\gamma$ from the observed longitude $\lambda_2$. $$\bar{\lambda}_2=\lambda_2-\gamma$$ I have built an epicycle parameter calculator that implements the solution we have just worked through.     Calculating Parameters for the sun

    In order to apply this procedure to the Sun, we need to observe the position of the Sun in the sky relative to the background stars. In Hipparchus’ time, however, the instruments needed to carry out such a measurement with the required accuracy were not available. As it turns out, though, the Sun’s path in the sky (the ecliptic) intersects the equator of Earth’s rotation at two fixed points called equinoxes. When the Sun is at either of these points, the day lasts approximately 12 hours for all observers. The equinox points can thus be shown to be exactly $180\degree$ apart. There are also two fixed points located $90\degree$ from the equinoxes, called solstices. At these points, the Sun reaches its furthest point north or south of the equator.

    One of the simplest instruments for determining the equinox or solstice is a vertical stick placed in the ground called a gnomon, used to measure the length of shadows at noon. The longest and shortest noon shadows of the year occur at the solstices. The equinox corresponds to a shadow halfway in length between the two solstice shadows.

    For the three observations, we may choose from any of the equinox and solstice points. Another way we can describe this procedure is as finding the length of the seasons. This is because the seasons are defined astronomically as starting at the respective solstice or equinox. We have the spring equinox, summer solstice, autumnal equinox and winter solstice, which each mark the start of the corresponding season of the year. So we can find the parameters for the sun’s epicycle from the length of two consecutive seasons.

    Setting the Epoch

    The values we have just obtained for the Sun’s epicycle parameters are determined at the date of the second observation. It would, however, be useful to refer all parameters to a standard date of our choice. This date is our epoch. I will choose January 1, 2001, 00:00 UTC as the epoch.

    Converting the mean anomaly and the mean longitude to epoch is simple. Since the mean anomaly is the position of the Sun on the epicycle, the mean longitude is the position of the epicycle on the deferent, and both motions are uniform, we can calculate the positions at epoch, given the speed and time, as follows. Let $\alpha$ be the mean anomaly, $\bar\lambda$ be the mean longitude, $t_2$ be the time of the second observation and $t_0$ be our epoch date/time. $$\begin{align}&\alpha_0=\alpha+\omega_e(t_0-t_2) \&\bar\lambda_0=\bar\lambda+\omega_d(t_0-t_2)\end{align}$$ The equation of center is a little different, since it does not represent uniform motion. To find the equation of center at epoch, we must make use of the sine rule identity from earlier. $$\frac{\sin(\gamma)}{\sin(\gamma+\alpha)}=r$$ Our goal is to solve for the equation of center $\gamma$ , given the radius $r$ and the mean anomaly $\alpha$. The first step is to expand the denominator using the sine addition identity to get

    $$\frac{\sin(\gamma)}{\sin(\gamma)\cos(\alpha)+\cos(\gamma)\sin(\alpha)}=r$$ After taking the reciprocal, simplifying then solving for $\gamma$, we get the following: $$\frac{\sin(\gamma)}{\cos(\gamma)}=\frac{\sin(\alpha)}{\frac1r -\cos(\alpha)}$$ which we can also rewrite as:

    $$ \tan(\gamma)= \frac{r\sin(\alpha)}{1 -r\cos(\alpha)}$$

    To find the equation of center at epoch, we now just input the value we found for the mean anomaly at epoch into the equation.

    Position of the sun

    To calculate the position of the Sun at a given time, the procedure is exactly the same as we just did for the epoch, but our starting point becomes the epoch itself. Let $\alpha_0$ and $\bar\lambda_0$ be the mean anomaly and mean longitude at epoch $t_0$ , and let $\alpha$ and $\bar\lambda$ be the corresponding values at the given time $t$. We have

    $$\alpha=\alpha_0 +\omega_e(t-t_0)$$ $$\bar\lambda=\bar\lambda_0+\omega_d(t-t_0)$$ We determine the equation of center using the same formula we derived: $$ \tan(\gamma)= \frac{r\sin(\alpha)}{1 -r\cos(\alpha)}$$ and the longitude of the moon is given by: $$\lambda=\bar\lambda+\gamma$$

    Parameters for the Moon

    To find the parameters for the Moon, we need three observations. You might expect that we would compare the Moon’s position with that of a body whose position is already known (the Sun, for example) and thereby infer the Moon’s position. However, the angle-measuring instruments of the time were not sufficiently accurate, so a different approach was necessary.

    Hipparchus therefore devised a more ingenious method for locating the Moon: he used lunar eclipses. During the middle of a lunar eclipse, the Sun and Moon are exactly opposite each other. If the Sun’s position at that moment is known, the Moon’s position follows immediately as the point directly opposite the Sun (by adding 180°). This method requires no additional instrumentation; knowing the time of the lunar eclipse is enough. As a result, Hipparchus could even use older Babylonian observations that recorded only the date and time of eclipses.

    Geometry of Eclipses

    To predict eclipses we will need to look at the circumstances under which an eclipse occurs. We will look at the geometry of the shadows formed during an eclipse, then show how this is to the position of the Moon relative to the node.

    Shadow geometry

    The following diagram shows a spherical light source of radius $R_L$ casting light on a spherical object of radius $R_O$. The light source is at a distance $d_L$. The shadow is then cast at a distance $d_S$ from the object. The shadow has two parts: the umbra, which is the darkest part, is created when the object completely blocks all light coming directly from the source, and the penumbra, which is the fuzzier part, is created where light is only partially blocked by the object. In some cases, there is also an antumbra, which begins where the umbra ends and is much lighter than the umbra.

    The case of the umbra forms two similar triangles, one with a base at the radius of the light source and the other with a base at the radius of the object. Let us label the distance from the center of the object to the vertex of the triangle as $x$.

    Since the triangles are similar, we know that the ratio of any sides should be the same. So the ratio of height to base for both triangles gives: $$\frac{d_L+x}{R_L}=\frac x{R_O}$$ We can now solve for $x$ as $$R_O(d_L+x)=xR_L$$ $$x(R_L-R_O)=R_Od_L$$ We get the distance to the vertex $x$ as: $$x=\frac{R_Od_L}{R_L-R_O}$$ We now want to determine the radius of the shadow $R_S$ formed at a given distance $d_S$ from the screen (Note if $d_S\ge x$ we are no longer in the umbra but the antumbra). To determine the radius of the shadow we note that it forms another similar triangle.

    We thus obtain the equation $$\frac{x-d_S}{R_S}=\frac x{R_O}$$

    We can cross-multiply to solve for $R_S$. $$R_S=\frac{R_O}x(x-d_S)$$ If our answer is negative, this means we are in the antumbra of the shadow. Ignore the negative sign and take that as the radius of the antumbra.

    We can now turn our attention the case of the penumbra. In this case we still have two similar triangles, but on opposite sides of the vertex, as seen in the diagram above. Our similar triangles give us the following equation $$\frac{d_L-y}{R_L}=\frac y{R_O}$$ We can solve for $y$ to get the vertex of the penumbra as $$R_O(d_L-y)=yR_L$$ $$y(R_L+R_O)=R_Od_L$$ $$y=\frac{R_Od_L}{R_L+R_O}$$ To determine the radius of the penumbra $R_P$ , we note that it also forms a similar triangle. We thus have $$\frac{y+d_S}{R_P}=\frac y{R_O}$$ $$R_P=\frac{R_O}x(x+d_S)$$ In astronomy, we are usually interested in the question of the apparent sizes of things when viewed from a distance. This is called the angular size. For our purposes we will use the following simple formula for angular size $\vartheta$. $$\vartheta = \frac{2R}d$$ where $\theta$ is the angular size in radians, $R$ is the radius of the object, and $d$ is the distance to the object from the point of observation.

    So far I haven’t made mention of how ancient astronomers would measure distances to the sun and moon. A future article in this series will be dedicated entirely to discussing the various methods used for determining these distances.

    Magnitude of an eclipse

    The magnitude of a lunar eclipse measures how immersed the moon is in the Earth’s shadow. More precisely, for a partial lunar eclipse, we define the magnitude as the fraction of the moon’s diameter that is covered by the Earth’s umbra.

    [diagram here] During a partial lunar eclipse, the moon and the Earth form two overlapping circles as shown in the diagram above. Let the moon be a circle of radius $R_O$ and the the Earth’s umbra be the circle with radius $R_S$, and the distance between the center of the two circles is $c$. The magnitude $M$ by definition is then $$M= \frac{R_O+R_S-c}{2R_O}$$ When $M \ge 1$, the moon is completely immersed in the umbra, we call this a total lunar eclipse. If $M \le 0$ , the Moon is outside the umbra, although it may still lie within the penumbra.

     The same idea defines the penumbral magnitude: we calculate it exactly as before, but using the penumbra instead. If the penumbral magnitude $M_p \le 0$, the Moon lies entirely outside the shadow, and no eclipse occurs.

    Position of the Nodes

    The two points where the solar orbits and lunar orbits intersect are called nodes. The following diagram shows the geometry of this intersection.

    [diagram here]

    The point $\Omega$ is the node, $\beta$ is called the latitude of the moon, and the angle $\iota$ is called the orbital inclination. $\lambda$ is the longitude of the moon, which we know how to determine. We also note the right angled triangle formed in the diagram. However, since the sides of this triangle is not formed by straight lines but arcs of circles, we must use the identities of spherical trigonometry to determine the relation between the sides. We can show that the following relation holds for the spherical triangle in the diagram.

    $$\sin\beta=\sin\iota\cdot\sin(\lambda-\Omega)$$ We already know how to determine the longitude of the moon. So we now need to know how to determine the other three quantities.

    The latitude $\beta$ is the angular distance between the center of the Moon and the ecliptic. This quantity can be determined at mid-eclipse, because it is becomes the same as the distance between the Moon and center of shadow $c$ we discussed earlier, but now $\beta$ is an angular distance, so we convert between them using the angular size formula: $$|\beta|= \frac cd$$ Note I have included $\beta$ in absolute value bars because angular size is strictly positive, whereas latitude is considered negative when it is below the ecliptic and positive when it is above the ecliptic. To determine the sign of the latitude during an eclipse we observe whether the Moon is above or below the center of the eclipse shadow, which is on the ecliptic.

    The orbital inclination $\iota$ is the angle at which the moon and the sun’s path intersect. This also equals the maximum latitude the Moon can achieve. From Astronomical Constants, we take the orbital inclination of the Moon as $i=5.145\degree$.

    The longitude of the node $\Omega$ is the longitude of the point where the ecliptic and the path of the moon intersects. There are two such points: the ascending node is the point where the moon’s latitude is increasing, and the descending node where the latitude is decreasing. While $\Omega$ could be any of the nodes, it is convenient to choose one of them. We go by the convention of taking $\Omega$ to be the longitude of the ascending node.

    To determine the correct quadrant for $\lambda-\Omega$, we need to know which node the Moon was at during the eclipse, which can be determined by whether the Moon’s latitude was increasing (ascending node) or decreasing (descending node).

    The Nodes at Epoch

    As we have mentioned before, the nodes are not fixed. They move in the opposite direction to the moon. The mean speed of the moon with respect to the node $\omega_n$ can be computed from the mean length of the draconic month ($27.212221$ days), and the mean speed of the moon with respect to the vernal equinox $w_d$ is computed from the mean length of the tropical month ( $27.321582$ days).

    For simplicity, we will assume the nodes move uniformly, so that the motion of the nodes is given by the mean motion: $$\Omega_0=\Omega+(\omega_n-\omega_d)(t-t_0)$$ where $\Omega_0$ is the longitude of the node at epoch, $\Omega$ is the longitude at the time of observation and $t$ is the time between the observation and epoch.

    Predicting Lunar Eclipses

    We now have everything ready to predict lunar eclipses. Lunar eclipses occur at full moon when the Moon is opposite the sun. Our algorithm for predicting eclipses thus begins by finding the full moon then calculating the eclipse geometry to determine whether a lunar eclipse occurs, and the type of eclipse.

    Finding the Full Moon

    We will create an algorithm to find the next full moon after a given time $t$. Let $\ell_S$ be the longitude of the Sun and $\ell_M$ be the longitude of the Moon. The elongation is defined as $$\varepsilon=\ell_M-\ell_S$$ If $\epsilon=180\degree$ then we have a full moon. We can also define the mean elongation, using the mean longitudes as: $$\bar{\varepsilon} = \bar{\ell}M - \bar{\ell_S}$$ The rate $\omega{\varepsilon}$ at which this changes can be found by subtracting the mean speed of the Sun from the mean speed of the Moon, or alternatively it can be computed directly from the length of the synodic month.

    Our algorithm procedes by a simple fixed point iteration. First, we compute the time $\Delta t$ required to increase the mean elongation $\bar\varepsilon$ from its current value to $180\degree$. $$\Delta t=\frac{180\degree-\bar\varepsilon}{w_\varepsilon}$$ However, our goal is to get the true elongation $\epsilon$ to be equal to $180\degree$. We first calculate the true elongation by inputting the time, along with the other parameters, into the epicycle models for the Sun and Moon and use the resulting longitudes to find the elongation. We note the difference between this longitude and $180\degree$ and use this to compute another time increment to push the elongation closer to $180\degree$. $$\Delta t=\frac{180\degree-\varepsilon}{\omega_\varepsilon}$$ After this step we note the elongation is now much closer to $180\degree$. Repeating this last step a few times, we converge very quickly to an elongation of $180\degree$. Thus we have found the exact time of the full moon, and we also have the longitudes of the Sun and Moon at that time.

    Classifying Solar Eclipses

    We can calculate the magnitude and the penumbral magnitude of the shadow in the same way we did earlier for lunar eclipses. However, because the shadow now falls on the Earth itself, the type of eclipse—and whether it can be seen at all—depends on where the observer stands within the shadow. An observer in the penumbra sees the Moon partially block the Sun’s light, which we call a partial eclipse. An observer in the umbra sees the Moon completely block the Sun’s light, producing a total eclipse. Finally, an observer in the antumbra also sees the Moon block the Sun’s light, but the Sun appears larger than the Moon, forming a ring of light around it; this is called an annular eclipse.
